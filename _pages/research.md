<center><b> The Behavioral Impact of Sulfur Fuel Content Regulations in Maritime Shipping </b> </center>

***Abstract***: In 2012, the US and Canada implemented a technological regulation, called the ECA (Emission Control Area), in their exclusive economic zone to reduce emissions from ocean-going ships. The ECA required ships to use low-sulfur fuel, increasing a ship’s fuel costs. In transportation, however, an increase in fuel cost will lead to changes in shipping supply characteristics such as operating speed, ship size, and frequency. This paper aims to quantify the extent of adjustment in maritime shipping supply characteristics in response to the ECA regulation. I first present a stylized model to highlight how the ECA affects shipping supply characteristics by changing the cost of fuel. I then test these predictions using port entrance and exit data from the US Army Corps of Engineers. I find that for the aggregate cost increase, treated routes saw ships that were, on average, about 15 percent larger in gross tons and had a 25-trip increase in the frequency of trips compared with similar, untreated routes, and ships on treated routes moved 16 percent slower than predicted in the imputed counterfactual. The size of the cost change matters, however, for the response to ship size. 


<center><b>Behavioral Responses to Information on PFAS in Drinking Water</b> </center>

***Abstract***: This paper estimates the effect of information provisions on the awareness and avoidance of PFAS exposure. Past research quantified how individuals avoid exposure to contaminants, such as E. coli or lead, when notified by measuring additional bottled water purchases. The case of PFAS is unique; the full health effects of PFAS are relatively uncertain, and PFAS stays in drinking water longer than other common contaminants. We leverage variation in violations across nine states with PFAS regulations between 2019 to 2023 to quantify these impacts. We employ a two-way fixed effects design, pairing detailed violation data at the weekly level with retail scanner data. Our results show bottled water expenditure increases by 7\% following the first PFAS violation and no significant increase for water filters. This increase represents an aggregate cost of approximately \$4 million for the first PFAS violation.
